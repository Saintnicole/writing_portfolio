Do you know who my father is?

Oh please, just shut up.

Published on Date • By Nicolletta Eneji • 5 min read

Let me begin by giving a loud and sincere shout-out to a very specific group of parents.

You see, when the book of Isaiah said, “Instead of your former shame, you will receive a double portion,” I am convinced it was written with you in mind. Because with the kind of children you are harbouring in your homes? You deserve every bit of that double portion. Hazard allowance included.

However, some of una no dey try for us.

You will clearly see the evidence of principalities and powers operating freely in your household—in the physical form of a child—and instead of addressing it properly, instead of sending that child to therapy, an asylum or, at best, Apostle Arome’s ministry, you will use your hard-earned millions to ship him off to a university where he can begin terrorizing the rest of us. Haba, Mummy?

You’re probably wondering why I have come with my wahala when the new month just started. Well, since I graduated and officially joined the Association of Unemployed Youths, I’ve been spending an unhealthy amount of time reminiscing about my university days. Last week, I reminisced a little too hard, and a particular irritating memory from my third year resurfaced.

Since then, I’ve been mildly annoyed at myself, fed up with certain parenting decisions, and honestly disgusted by the children they produce.

I spoke to David for the first time in my third year; he was a Software Engineering student, just like me. I had seen him around campus countless times but never interacted with him—largely because I had absolutely no reason to. He was not my kind of person, and our paths never naturally crossed. There was no connection whatsoever—until Ezra.

Ezra, his closest friend, became my friend shortly before the second semester of 300 Level. And David, who had clearly been waiting for an opportunity, saw that friendship as an open window to stride into my life.

Yes, stride.

No introduction. No greeting. No shame.

He took one long, confident step to where Ezra and I were seated and said, “This babe, I like you.”

That was our first interaction.

Normally, I would’ve hissed and relocated. It was a clear sign of foolishness by my standards, but I overlooked it. Ezra had become a good friend, and it seemed harsh to write off his closest friend immediately. I convinced myself that anyone Ezra kept close couldn’t be completely unhinged. Ezra himself had sense and I believed some of it would’ve rubbed off.

I was wrong.

Three weeks after that encounter, Ezra invited my friend Naza and me to his house-warming party. I was skeptical. I am a committed avoider of anything that begins with, “You know we’re all adults here...” But Ezra assured me that it was going to be nothing like that. In fact, he bribed me with my favourite drink—Sosa (I hate it now, by the way). And that was how I succumbed, forgetting to make him promise that I wouldn’t have to indulge unwell people.

A few minutes after I settled in, David walked toward me wearing the most aggressively ripped jeans I had ever seen. The jeans were hanging by strands of thread in different sections. He looked at me, smiled, sat beside me, and like the first time, the next thing that came out of his mouth was, “I like you.”

Only this time, his voice was slower, calmer, more audacious, and—weed? I could perceive weed. That explained the level of audacity and aura farming. No wonder he sat there like a Bollywood star waiting for some slo-mo music after that careless declaration for the second time.

It was the weed.

And do you know where I messed up? I made excuses for him again because he was Ezra’s friend—not because I liked him. In fact, David was not my kind of guy at all. I liked men that looked clean—no fuzzy hair, well put together, smart outfits, and intelligent speech. David was the opposite.

But I rated Ezra too much to accept that his closest friend might simply be a confused alte student. I believed strongly in “birds of a feather flock together," and admitting David was unwell would mean questioning Ezra too. I wasn’t ready for that.

After the party, Ezra, Naza, and I grew closer. And of course, Ezra’s closest friend wasn’t going to disappear. David inserted himself into the group as well—closer to me than Naza. He never missed an opportunity to remind me that he liked me, and each time, I calmly reminded him that we were only ever going to be friends.

With no intention to throw a shade, I like intelligent men. What would I do with a man who was always talking about buying drip and getting his invincible bread up?

I didn’t tell him that part though. He had a very fragile ego. World War III would break out if I dared to open my mouth.

Slowly, David became a pain in my ass without me realizing on time. During lectures, he would sulk and ignore the class entirely whenever I sat with my other male friends—the ones who actually understood what was going on in class.

If I spoke to him afterward, he would respond like a landlord whose tenant had defaulted on rent, as though his father contributed to my tuition, and I was breaching contract by sitting elsewhere.

He even claimed one of my closest friends was using intelligence to bag babes—myself included.

Sir?

I had just gotten a GPA of 5.0 for the third time in a row. Exactly how does one intend to impress me with what I already have in excess? Also, I believe you have a brain too? What stopped you from bagging babes with it?

Then David said something that summarized his entire mindset. He asserted that most intelligent people don’t end up being successful and wealthy in life. He used his father as an example—an average student, but he turned out to be a wealthy man.

“All this book...book...book…you people will end up working for people that don’t even know the book.”

What a poetic way to package the apology of a CGPA that was sitting boldly on his student portal.

Now to the main tea, where the madness really happened.

Many people in my school had wealthy parents. If you wanted to chase a girl, you needed more “doings” than the next man chasing her. You couldn’t just show up with an Afro and jeans that looked like they survived a civil war. You’ll remain single till the second coming of Jesus.

David knew he didn’t have enough "doings", so he relied on one thing.

His father.

According to him, his father was a general in the Nigerian Army, and he made sure to echo it in the ears of anyone who was unfortunate enough to cross paths with him. He announced it so much that if you didn’t have substantial knowledge, you would think his father was the only General in the Federal Republic of Nigeria.

Every argument, every disagreement, every inconvenience, always ended with, “Do you know who my father is?”

Or my personal favourite:

“I almost sent my father’s soldiers to deal with them.”

Egbon, did your father transfer ownership of the barracks into your name?

I had to do some Google research to find out if his father handed over the Nigerian army to him as a birthday present. The audacity was too loud to be mere babbling.

And the worst part? He genuinely believed his father’s office gave him authority. The boy spoke like he had the immunity of the GCFR when he had not one degree to his name.

Mid-semester exams came, and Dr. Aisha wrote two A4 pages worth of instructions on the course group. The most important instruction was highlighted boldly: “BRING ALTERNATIVE INTERNET CONNECTION. THE SCHOOL WIFI MAY BE DOWN.”

Everybody read that line except David, who walked into the hall with baggy jeans, a laptop, and no internet source. The Wi-Fi was down, as she had predicted, and that was where the problem started.

David had to ask around for people’s passwords until Chibuike, my friend, agreed. He connected and started the exam—then, Chibuike submitted and left the hall. David was stranded. He sat there helpless until time ran out and the submission portal closed. He met me outside the hall with smoke practically erupting from his ears and nose.

“I will beat the hell out of this Chibuike boy.”

At least, it was him that would beat the boy, not his father’s soldiers. That was an improvement.

“What happened?”

“The boy knew I was connected to his internet, yet he left the hall early to go outside and talk to a babe. I couldn’t get another source before the deadline, and now, the portal has closed.”

I was with Chibuike shortly after the exam, so I knew that wasn’t true. Chibuike had not rushed out for a babe; he had simply forgotten that David was still connected.

And even if he had—why should your lack of internet be a hindrance to Chibuike’s love life? Chibuike was not David’s friend; he didn’t owe him generosity or loyalty. What was the entitlement for? Why didn’t you bring a router with all the wealth you make noise about?

David hated that I was defending Chibuike, so as usual, he hissed and left. Some days later, he told me he went to the lecturer’s office to beg but she threatened to call security.

Then he said, “Do you know what I told her?”

I asked, “What?”

“I asked her, ‘Do you know who my father is?’”

She still threw him out without caring about his father’s status.

Eventually, when she gave a makeup exam to the few who were unable to submit, he failed—because he had to write in her office with no aid from ChatGPT.

He blamed Chibuike again for ruining his opportunity, and blamed me even more for supporting Chibuike.

Still in the exam season, but on a different day, one of the lecturers gave us an assignment to be completed in less than 24 hours, using an app we had never been taught how to navigate. As expected, David did not even install the app. I didn’t blame him though; more than half of the department also had intentions to modify and submit what their friends had achieved.

But that day, David and I had a misunderstanding and he became rude and uncouth. He only calmed down when he realized he hadn’t submitted the assignment and couldn’t afford to jeopardize his only hope of securing a decent score. So he apologized and asked if I could still send it.

I did.

David had a knack for promising girls meagre fortunes. He would offer to buy you food, offer to clear your Shein cart, offer to take you on a date—mostly because he knew you would say, “No, thank you.”

How do I know this?

The one time I said yes, I noticed his pace became slower. Anxious. When we got to the food stand, the cashier returned his card and said, with a straight face, “Declined.” I paid for my food eventually.

On another day, he said he was hungry and I told him to order food for himself. He said, “My money is not to be spent by me, it is yours. If you want me to eat, you’ll have to buy the food for me.”

No go warm eba chop.

The Port-harcout man thought I was senseless. He wanted me to spend my allowance on his food and that fat delivery fee, while his own card was declining for common loaded fries.

Thief.

On one of my maintenance Fridays, David asked where I was and I told him I had gone to the salon. He said, “This girl, you have money o.” then asked, “How much should I contribute?”

Do I run an ajo business?

Abi I skipped the part where I mentioned that my money is insufficient?

Men that make the kind of noise you make usually say, “How much is the bill?”

And you’re aking me how much you should “contribute”?

Omo. Please just go and sit down.

The day I had exhausted my tolerance for David’s nonsense, I sent him a text ending the friendship. I offered academic help, but I couldn’t afford to be described in close association with him when girls eventually gathered to narrate events. “The baggy jeans boy that is always around Nicole.” That would be a stain on my white.

He asked why, and who badmouthed him. I told him I was just tired, but he pressed until I said, “You’re not adding value to my life.”

The next message I saw was:

“If you can’t say it to my face, don’t chat shit to me, lol.”

Excuse me?

I left the message unattended and stopped speaking to him.

He sent friends to plead, with Ezra claiming the boy was on the verge of suicide. I wasn’t buying it. Suicidal people don’t make noise; They leave a note and drink sniper. E reach David turn, he dey drop press release. Please abeg.

During exams, he cornered me at the only dependable place I could be found—the library. He stood there smiling with a notebook, expecting me to teach him. I ignored for two days until the third. He came with red, teary eyes. He claimed he “didn’t mean” the text the way I interpreted it. I knew there was no alternative interpretation, but for the sake of peace, I accepted his apology.

He offered penance by paying for my dinner. I politely refused. He insisted. When we got to the food court, his card declined. Again. I paid for my food and returned to my hostel, asking God, “biko kedu ka m siri rute ebe a?”

How did I get here lord?

When David realized he was taking too many Ls, he needed redemption. Especially after hyping my birthday for a month, only to ask “Should I buy you a gift?” when the day came.

He noticed he was becoming irritating, and his ultimate plan was to pull up in his father’s AMG.

“Howfar Nicole, where are you?”

“I’m studying Data Communications.”

“Oh, I’m not offering the course. Let’s go to yellow plate.”

But I was offering the course?

David insisted and promised we wouldn’t spend more than thirty minutes. I started wondering if he did yahoo overnight—because the same David whose card declined for N3,500 loaded fries could not possibly be planning to take me to yellow plate.

When I got to the parking lot, I saw the real reason. He didn’t want yellow plate’s milkshake, he wanted me to see the car. No wonder, he kept swinging the key in my face so I would catch the hint that, “THIS IS LUXURY LIVING✨”.

I suggested Jabi Lake mall instead—mainly to protect my own pocket when his card eventually failed. He drove like a typical Abuja boy, making unnecessary gunshot sounds from the V8 engine.

At the mall, he asked if I wanted to empty racks:

“Should we enter Mango?”

“What about Keluxe?”

“Lacoste?”

“Miniso? You want pink frying pan?”

He asked so frequently that I almost thought I had misjudged him; then, we got to KFC.

“Nicole what would you like to eat.”

“Just chicken.”

“You don’t want to spend my money? Okay. How many pieces?”

“Plenty.”

His eyes widened. “Give me three pieces.”

Upon all the noise?

The cashier said,“That will be N9,600.”

“Ahn, remove one piece.”

Brother Daviiiid???

David called them thieves for selling chicken at that price. So the yellow plate he was hyping—did they offer buy now, pay never? I felt secondhand embarrassment, hoping his card would at least work this time. I asked if he wasn’t getting anything, and he said he wasn’t hungry.

Lamba.

He begged for my chicken in the car. He just didn’t want his bill to increase.

On our way back, he resumed his fast and furious audition. I told him repeatedly to slow down because I refused to die over two pieces of chicken.

Then he swerved into a filling station and told the attendant, “N3,000 fuel.”

N3,000 fuel. For a V8 engine?

Let’s not forget that one piece of chicken was around N3,000. The same piece he told them to remove from the order. So I started connecting dots. Was his account balance so precise that the extra chicken would’ve left us trekking?

And for context, this was not during Jonathan’s Nigeria. Not Buhari’s either. This was City boy’s tenure, after fuel subsidy had been lifted and prices were doing a marathon.

If David had actually taken me to yellow plate, who would have paid? Me?

Omo mehn.

When realization finally settled in, my energy shifted. I sat there irritated while he smiled throughout the ride, genuinely thinking he had impressed me with those childish engine sounds.

At a traffic light, he pulled out his phone and scrolled to a picture he had shown me weeks before. His uncle’s G-wagon. He turned the screen toward me and said with full confidence, “This is now your car oo.”

Lame nigga with zero game.

Any microscopic possibility of resurrection for that friendship died that afternoon. I distanced myself completely, but he assumed I was just occupied because of my exams. During the final exam week, there was a public holiday—so, we had no paper that day.

David called to ask where I was, and I told him I was reading in my hostel.

“I’ll be there in twenty minutes.”

You’ll be where? did you not hear that I’m reading?

“It won’t be more than fifteen minutes.”

I had an exam the next morning, and I hadn’t performed well in my midterms. I could bet my next school fees on the fact that he hadn’t either. But because he had stolen his father’s keys again, he urgently felt the need to come and parade horsepower.

I refused firmly, only to receive a notification:

“I’m at the parking lot.”

What exactly are you doing there?

The boy was hell-bent on impressing me with the car, even after I clearly said I was unavailable. I told him I wasn’t leaving my hostel. Then, he started complaining about the distance he had driven all the way from Asokoro to Idu.

Did I send you?

Less than ten minutes later, he sent a snap of another girl seated in the passenger seat. He thought hiding her face was mysterious enough. It wasn’t.

I knew those white toenails. I knew those unmistakable slippers.

The genius had sent me a snap of my roommate in an attempt to spark jealousy—and in his infinite wisdom, he assumed cropping her upper body would make her unrecognizable.

I didn’t respond. I waited until exams ended and informed my roommate, who was completely oblivious to the role she had unknowingly played in his mediocre movie production. We both removed him on every app, and blocked him where he needed to be blocked.

As expected, he started his sympathy campaign again. He sent his friends to inform me of his suicidal thoughts and weightloss.

You don’t have money to eat. Let’s start there. And you’re dispatching people to tell me that you’re starving because you miss me?

I di oke nzuzu o. You must be very silly.

When his emotional blackmail failed, he reached for his last card—the “refund” speech.

He thanked me for everything and said he felt obligated to return every kobo I had spent on him. The first time he used that line during a disagreement, it unsettled me. Gesture receipts aren’t meant to be kept (at least, by decent people). What exactly was he refunding?

But this time? I was unmoved.

I sent my account number without hesitation. And to this day—4th March 2026, the refund David passionately promised in March 2024 is still in transit.

David finally reached out on imessage where I forgot to block him. He announced that a prophet had seen a vision of him becoming a powerful politician. He added that I might need his help in future, so he didn’t understand why I was gambling with destiny.

I blocked the werey on imessage.

Dear parents, your child does not need to attend coven meetings or start eliminating people before you realize something is spiritually and morally off. Some demons manifest as chronic stupidity and questionable character—and this was clearly one of those cases. If you notice early symptoms, please find your way to Apostle Arome for deliverance. The rest of us did not enroll to be part of the rehabilitation program.

As we speak, David is somewhere promising another girl an iPhone 17 pro max and a cleared Shein cart. I just hope her bank card is strong enough to rescue her when his inevitably declines.
