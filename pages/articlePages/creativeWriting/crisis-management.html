You still haven't learnt crisis management in big 2026?

Hop on, let Nicole teach you.

Published on February 16 2026• By Nicolletta Eneji • 5 min read

There’s one proven way to cope with havoc in this country, and that is by dragging as many innocent people into it as possible. Most learn this as adults, but my secondary school instilled this survival tactic into my brain before I even knew the English language had more than five vowel sounds.

It was Pentecost Sunday—the one sacred day when Catholic students were allowed a break from silent meditations in exchange for the full Pentecostal package: miracles, dramatic falling, aggressive tongues, and the Holy Ghost fishing out both the called and the possessed. I longed for it just as much as every other girl in the hall pretending not to be interested.

This particular Pentecost was extra special because it fell right after visiting day. That meant more strength to pray because of Saturday’s feast, and more importantly, more provisions to quench the hunger that inevitably followed a three-hour warfare session.

Mrs Agu, the church matron, marched in with three men from the local parish. They looked unfriendly, possessing the kind of faces that would make the devil jump up from a nightmare. I wondered why she had brought such stiff-faced men to an all-girls' school until she grabbed the mic.

“We will be performing an exorcism.”

Exorcism kwa? What is exorcism?

I was trying to process the vocabulary when one of the men snapped me out of my thoughts. “Some people in this room carry spirits from the kingdom of darkness.”

Obviously! I thought. If there weren’t people like that, my N200 wouldn’t have disappeared from my locker on Friday night.

The man seemed to be guessing, and I hated it. I’ve always had low tolerance for vague miracles; if you have power, call names and shake tables. Which one is “some people…"? Please shift!

I rolled my eyes, wondering where Mrs Agu had found these unserious men, until Ene flew.

Not metaphorically.

She flew.

Three rows back.

And landed on a stack of chairs.

Ahn ahn? Ene my guy?

“Leave me alooone!!” she wailed in a deep masculine bass. The Ene I knew sounded more feminine than Ariana Grande on a heartbreak track; this had to be a spiritual voiceover.

“I said leave that body alone!” the man shouted back, marching towards Ene, who was making a break for the exit. He ordered that all the doors be locked firmly. According to him, those with foul spirits would attempt escape, and today was judgement day.

Wonderful.

Ene began to slither on the floor as he blasted in tongues. I didn’t just get goosebumps; I began to cry—especially when the girl beside me whispered, “And she’s Nicole’s friend o!”

Which Nicole? And since when did Ene become her friend?

I denied Ene the same way Peter denied Jesus, and even if a cock crowed ten times, I wasn’t going to feel a shred of remorse.

My tears entered a new stage—the one that comes with rethinking your life choices. Why? Because I had collected cream crackers from her on Saturday night. The same night she ate the rice that started this spiritual drama.

“I will destroy everyone!” Ene roared from the altar as they battled for her soul.

Everyone?

The “everyone” would obviously start with me. I had eaten biscuits from a spiritually compromised person; what more did it take to destroy someone? My tears advanced to the ultimate stage—calculating how to relocate countries.

Thankfully, many girls were crying under the influence of the Holy Spirit, so I blended in seamlessly. Tears were universal; nobody could tell if they were from spiritual brokenness or pure panic.

But then, I had to take action.

I needed to exit that church without anyone suspecting I was part of Ene’s demonic franchise. More importantly, I needed to distribute my own biscuits to the students who had not yet heard the breaking news.

Witchcraft was not uncommon in our school, but there was a code. If the spirit in you cooperated and kept quiet, you’d make it through the term without the “possessed girl” stigma. But if people found out you were compromised? It was social suicide. Nobody would speak to you. Nobody would sit near you. Nobody would borrow from you. Nobody would even tell you answers during exams—which meant submitting a blank script for chemistry. God forbid.

The worst part is the stigma applied to your friends too. In a school where begging was the norm, they’d assume your friends had been initiated through whatever you shared. I had to move fast. If nobody collected biscuits from me, and it turned out Ene had truly initiated me without consent, then I would be in this alone. I was planning to run for deputy head girl the following term, and demonic allegations were not part of my campaign strategy.

I turned to Jenny, the warden assigned to my row. “How far, Jenny? I’m purging from the food I ate yesterday. You know Dami’s mother brought soured awara; I need to use the toilet.”

“I will expose everyone!!!!” Ene roared again.

Why Ene go do me like this, abeg?

“Jenny, please be quick! I need to run to the hostel.”

“Mrs Agu said the doors must remain shut.”

The same girl who begged me to sneak in contraband the night before had suddenly become the patron saint of compliance. Overnight she rediscovered the rulebook and somehow found the audacity to quote Mrs Agu to me.

“Jenny, please,” I whispered desperately.

Then I saw Ene’s face.

It wasn’t green, nor was it physically transformed. But her features had rearranged themselves into something that resembled a goblin from Merlin.

Chai, Ene.

Is this what I will look like?

This girl has ruined my political future.

I needed a new strategy. I needed to be out. Then, inspiration came.

“Jenny, if you don’t let me use the toilet now, I will tell Mrs Agu and the hostel matron that you have a phone. And tha you’re always calling that boy in CKC. His name is Emma, abi?”

Her eyes widened. She looked at me like Jesus must’ve looked at Judas during the Last Supper, but what was my business? One lost friendship was better than being isolated until graduation. I was running for deputy head girl, and I couldn’t afford these kinds of stains.

Eventually, she showed me a hidden exit through the sacristy. She tried multiple keys until one finally turned. The door opened. I was free.

I rushed to the hostel, opened my box, and took out the carton of Pure Bliss. I tore it open with urgency and began to share.

“Ahn ahn, Nicole, is it your birthday?” the girls asked, shocked to see the stingiest girl in the hostel distributing wafers.”

“Yes, happy birthday to me!” I lied. Foolish girls. The last time I had a birthday, I didn’t even give them pure water. Yet, here they were, thinking I was sharing my favourite snacks out of the goodness of my heart. At least, their foolishness was in my favour.

When the Pure Bliss finished, I moved to the cream crackers, then Capri-Sun. I shared everything until the initiation was widespread. I skipped the spiritual girls and pastor’s daughters. Those ones could eat directly from Lucifer’s kitchen and stay holy. I wasn’t wasting my provisions on them.

I realized my hostel wasn’t enough. My political ambition required wide coverage. I used my pocket money at the hostel kiosk to buy more, then toured Pearl House, Ruby, Emerald, and finally Amethyst—Ene’s hostel, where I didn’t have to distribute much because the Queen Mother had likely finished the job there.

By the time I returned, my box contained only bathing soap and toilet paper.

“Ene will ask God for bread, and he’ll give her dog meat.” I muttered in pain, staring at my empty box.

That night, I slept hoping for one of two outcomes: that I didn’t enter another realm or that I entered with 70% of the student population.

Ene was fine in the weeks that followed. The strong men had apparently dealt with that demon, but the stigma stuck to her. She was isolated by everyone, including me, “her guy”.

My anger was deep. My provisions had vanished for nothing. There was no initiation, and I wasn’t a witch. I was just a hungry girl with DHG aspirations. My friendship with Ene had cost me heavily. Even if the stigma wore off, I was not going to forgive that girl.

Hunger eventually called me to wild action, so I gathered Jenny and Dami for a hostel raid. If Ene’s coven wouldn’t kill me, hunger would. It was my only option.

We moved from hostel to hostel, inspecting junior students’ boxes as if the principal had personally commissioned us. I confiscated chocolates from a JSS2 girl who cried that they were from Ireland.

“And so what? Don’t you know that this school is anti-diabetes?” I snapped. “Is your mother the first to go to Ireland, abi you think you’re better than me because I eat parago?”

I punished her for the audacity.

We yielded three “Ghana-must-go” bags of provisions and divided the loot 50:25:25. Obviously, I took the lion’s share because if it weren’t for my brilliant idea, where would Dami and Jenny have seen Irish chocolates in this life?

That night, Jenny and I feasted on seized sardines and foreign chocolates like warriors who had just acquired spoils of war. Everything was perfect—until midnight. Footsteps and screams woke me up.

“Another one don start!” someone shouted.

Another what?

Jenny.

The same Jenny I had dined with six hours ago was manifesting.

She, who weighed less than 50kg, lifted an iron locker into the air and flung it at her roommate.

I stood there, frozen, hoping that whatever was wrong with her did not come from the sardine.

God. I hate this school.
