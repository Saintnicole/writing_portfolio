Maybe you're supposed to be my wife

Are you familiar with thunder?

Published on April 04 2026 • By Nicolletta Eneji • 9 min read

I am not publishing this newsletter on the exact day the nonsense happened, mostly because I needed time to translate my thoughts into words that are salvation-compliant... to a reasonable extent.

Yet, the moment my fingers hit my keyboard, that same Sunday irritation rose again—fresh, heavy, and fully prepared to express itself.

But do I blame myself? Absolutely not.

How could I possibly take the fall for ‘forgetting’, when four solid hours of boredom and unnecessary movement were involved?

And do I blame my darling Iya, who decided to spend the better part of her Sunday attending every church meeting known to man? Also, no.

I blame that invitation card.

I blame that small treacherous piece of stationery that decided to go missing after the 10:00 am mass two Sundays ago. If it had just stayed in one place, I wouldn’t have been back at the parish by 3:00 pm, and I certainly wouldn’t have run into that confused, overconfident Sunday school teacher who had the gall to look me in the face and say, "Let's go on a det.”

No, that’s not a typo. Your eyes read right, and my fingers are simply reporting the phonetic atrocity my ears heard. He said, “Let’s go on a det”.

Now, how did we get here?

Lately, I’ve been going to church on my own because it’s more convenient. I don’t have the patience to wait for my sister to finish painting her lip combo, or for my mum to finish constructing her gele with architectural precision.

I just get dressed, grab my bag, and off I go. Sharp.

But two Sundays ago, I decided to follow my mother. That was my first mistake.

I should have known it was a bad decision when the first three dresses I tried on refused to cooperate. Eventually, one did, and we left for church. I sat upstairs, while my mom and sister sat downstairs.

After mass, I came down, bag in hand, hoping we would go home after my mom finished greeting half of the congregation. But no. She told me to wait ‘a bit’ because she wanted to attend a meeting.

Meeting?

If there are forty societies in that church, my mother is in at least thirty-one. So when she says “a meeting”, just come with a pillow and a mat. At least five societies will hold their meetings concurrently or back-to-back, and my mother WILL show her face in all of them. No fatigue.

I told her I wanted to go home, having zero intention of spending my entire Sunday in church. She promised it would be brief, handed me an invitation card, and asked me to take it to the car.

I should have known better.

I should have known that the same woman who says she only wants to buy pepper, then proceeds to spend five hours in the market, is incapable of a ‘brief’ meeting. Her haste to hand over the invitation card should have been the sign, but I wasn’t paying attention.

I waited two hours for this “brief” meeting, but my mother was nowhere to be found.

Three hours. Still nothing.

Then, at three hours and forty-two minutes, there she was, strolling to the car like time had not moved. And she still stopped every four steps to greet people whose voices she recognized.

By then, I had meandered through every inch of that church. I bought puff-puff; I bought biscuits; I bought kunu aya. I even bought cake from one kiosk woman who attends mass every day but clearly lacks all the Fruits of the Spirit.

When my mother eventually reached the car, she came with snacks to pacify us. We hit H-Medix for groceries and finally got home around 3:20 pm. The moment I switched into housegirl mode, my mother asked, “Where’s the card.”

“Which card?”

She glared at me.

“That card better not be missing.”

I didn’t remember what card she was talking about...until I did. And I had absolutely no idea where it was.

“It’s in your car,” I hoped. But my hope came out as a statement. I prayed the Holy Spirit would pity me and manifest it there, but when my mom returned from checking the car, there was nothing except keys in her hand.

I’m finished.

“It’s not there!” Her voice had risen. “You better go and find that card! I gave you something to hold for a few minutes, and you can’t find it?”

Apparently, four hours is now a few minutes.

“I’m sure it’s in the car.”

She shoved the car keys at me. “Oya, go and bring it.”

I went. I checked. But there was nothing. When I returned empty-handed, my mother didn’t need a prophet to tell her that I had misplaced the card. Her tongue became sharp like a razor blade.

“You’ll not sit in one place!”

“You’ve misplaced that card, and I don’t even know the content!”

“When they tell you to do something, you won’t do it well!”

“You never remember anything unless it’s your own.”

Why didn’t I just go to another church?

“It’s you that will tell the woman you misplaced that card. I’m not going to disgrace myself!”

And the final one:

“I don’t know if that’s how you’ll be misplacing things in your husband’s house!”

When she realized shouting will not magically produce the card, she told me to wear my slippers and change out of my housegirl attire; because we were going to retrace my steps.

We started with H-Medix, but there was no sign. Even if the card had fallen there, whoever found it would assume there was money inside and zoom off with an invitation to an event he was not invited to.

Then, we went back to church.

I stepped down from the car before she parked, ready to begin my search. The moment I stepped through the church gate, a loud and familiar voice boomed.

“Are you looking for me?”

Unless your name is Invitation Card, please vacate my presence.

“No!” I replied.

“So what are you looking for?”

“A card”

“What kind of card?”

At this point, I already knew the man would not be helpful. He was just draining my time with unnecessary questions.

“It’s an invitation card,” I said, marching ahead.

“Slow down na; we’re searching together.”

Together kwa? Who involved you?

I didn’t slow down. but he matched my pace. This uncle did everything except actually look for that card. Instead, he launched into a conversation that was completely useless to my current crisis.

“So where are you serving now?”

“I’m not serving yet.”

“Ahn ahn. I thought you had graduated.”

“I have. I’m just not serving yet.”

He paused... then asked, with a hint of condescension, “What did you even study?” As if he was expecting Pure Water Education.

“I studied S o f t w a r e E n g i n e e r i n g.” I enunciated every syllable so it could penetrate his skull.

His eyes widened briefly. “That means you can build apps.”

“Yeah. I try.”

He looked unimpressed.

Let me give a quick lore on this man.

Uncle Osaze was my Sunday school teacher years back—and as a child, he annoyed me to my marrow.

I wasn’t the finest of children—unless you were counting from the back. And this man? He had a gift. A very profound gift for spotting only the prettiest girls whenever it was time to distribute Caprisonne and biscuits.

Caprisonne hardly ever reached me when he was the one sharing. If I stretched out my hand, he would claim it was for “six years and below”—when I was seven.

When I turned eight, the criteria suddenly shifted to “seven years and below”.

Very calculated wickedness.

Fast forward to my late teens—I started glowing up. I returned from boarding school with confidence and clear skin, and suddenly, this same man, this agent of Caprisonne deprivation became jovial toward me.

“Ahn Ahn... my friend, I hope school didn’t stress you.”

Who is your friend? Are your eyes failing you?

He made it a personal assignment to call me ‘my friend’ whenever he saw me. Loudly, too, so that witnesses would be present. Then, I entered university; every single time this man saw me in church, it was: “Are you done now?”

Done with what exactly? Did I enroll for a one-year diploma in Singing?” He asked that question during every holiday, every break, every opportunity he had to open his mouth. Until one Sunday, he said his mind:

“This your school don tey o.”

He said it as if I was repeating years. As if I had a mountain of carryovers to clear, and that was the last straw. I was only tolerating him because we attended the same church, but why was he behaving like my father borrowed money from him to pay my school fees?

From that day, I started avoiding him intentionally. If I saw him on the left, I turned right. If he was upstairs, I relocated downstairs. Very tactical movement.

Until last Sunday… when that card disappeared and dragged me back into his orbit.

Back to where I was

“You try,” He said, then continued, “What did you even graduate with?”

That has been my favourite question since last year.

“I graduated with a FIRST CLASS.”

I stressed it. I saw the surprise on his face, and it lingered a little too long. I stood there, waiting, because I knew this man’s superpower was asking foolish questions.

Right on cue: “So are you in a serious relationship.”

I expected foolishness, but not this kind.

“What does that even mean?”

“Yes na. This degree of yours means you’re hot cake. Nobody should play with you. You know these boys in your age group like to play.”

What kind of agbaya speech is that one?

This same man who taught me in Sunday school when I was a child was standing here, spewing rubbish in the house of God.

He continued boldly: “You’ve known me for long and I’m still not married.”

Truly a “you” problem, bruh.

I reminded him that I was still busy looking for my card. I didn’t come out in this Sunday heat just to listen to the delusions of an aging man. But overgrown men are famously dense. This loud uncle, who should be at a PTA meeting for his two children, was still following me around.

Card—you no help me find.

Caprisonne—you no give me that year.

Memory verse—you no gree make I recite.

And now that I’m a baddie 💆🏾‍♀️… you’re telling me that you’re unmarried?

Sir, I will lift you up in prayers. But that situation has absolutely nothing to do with me.

Then he said it:

“I’m very serious about you. You’ve known me since you were young.”

Exactly. And that is precisely why this conversation is disturbing.

“Don’t you want to be my friend?”

“In church, I know you.” I replied.

“Stop that thing! You want to brozone me”

He took it further. “Maybe God kept me unmarried because of you.”

Onwe ihe na-eme gi? I nyuru kai kai?

“I want to take you out. Let’s go on a det.”

“Ehn?”

I heard him. I just needed confirmation that my ears were still functioning correctly.

“I said, I want to take you out on a det. Give me your number, so we can talk about it.”

The way he pronounced ‘date’, I already knew the vision he had in mind—some palm wine and tilapia joint where they play loud highlife and call it ‘ambience’✨. It could never be a proper place. Never. Because that’s the pattern with older men pursuing younger girls. Very predictable.

I gave him my number so he would leave, but he didn’t. He followed me all the way to where my mother was standing with the catechist, acting like a concerned church worker helping a distressed girl. The catechist looked at us and asked who I was, then gave him a look that clearly said, “This is the first time I’m seeing you with this one.”

I didn’t blame him. Even I had seen Uncle Osaze rotating around different women after mass like a mobile network searching for connection. Bold of him to think I would be the one to end his year of marital wandering. He should continue looking for who to deceive.He doesn’t know that this generation has sense.

Anyway, we never found the card. And my mom later discovered that the event took place a few days after the card went missing. I wasted my entire Sunday looking for a scrap of paper and entertaining nonsense for absolutely nothing.

When I got home, he called. But my phone was on DND as always. I saw the number and immediately blocked it. Then the following Sunday, he saw me again, with my mother. Instead of minding his business, he walked up to me and said, “Stop hiding your face.”

Hide my face ke? I dey fear you?

I told him plainly that I wasn’t hiding my face, but I also did not want any interaction with him. He was shocked by my bluntness, and walked away—quietly.

Rubbish!
