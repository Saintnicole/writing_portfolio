Train your parents at home or I will train them for you

And I won’t be nice if I have to offer free training.

Published on May 16, 2026 • By Nicolletta Eneji • 6 min read

Today, every being in this realm stood in a jaw-dropped salutation on my account. Only the Nigerian Association of Untrained Parents (NAUPA) remained seated in their shame. And that’s how they will be seated until I am done sanitizing this nation of untrained elders.

My father has always been a man of impromptu emergencies. He has never been one to provide instructions or news with the luxury of time.

“Please edit this document; I need to send it by 9:00 am,” he would say at 8:15.

“Get ready for 8:00am mass; my friend has a child dedication,” he would announce at 7:23.

“I need my breakfast ready by 7:00,” he’d shout while knocking on your door at 5:00am.

So it did not surprise me when he asked us to get ready for a welcome party thirty minutes before the time. It was annoying, but unless I suddenly found the millions to cover my tuition, I had no choice but to keep up.

As usual, we were late. The typical threat of “I will leave whoever is not ready” was flying around the house while the announcer hadn’t even found his shoes.

The atmosphere was a war zone:

“Someone, lace my corset!”

“Where is my wig straightener?”

“Who stole my foundation?!”

“In fact, I am not going again!”

But somehow, through the chaos, we all looked stunning by the time we entered the car.

We arrived at the venue twenty minutes late, which, in Nigerian time, was basically early. Raindrops dotted some of the seats, as there was a downpour earlier, and the canopies did little to shield them. My father led us to the least soaked row, and my mother passed her hand towel around like a relay baton so we could dry our seats.

Shortly after we settled, a couple arrived: a very slim man and a robust woman, both around the age of fifty if I were to take a wild guess. They performed the classic Nigerian greeting routine—hugging some, shaking hands with others, and waving at people they barely knew. When the pleasantries were over, they came under the canopy to find seats, and they noticed the chairs were drenched.

The man and his wife walked straight to our row and greeted my parents. The man took the seat beside my sister, the extra one she had cleaned earlier. Then his wife looked my sister square in the face and told her to stand up and move to the chair beside her husband.

Yes, the wet seat.

“Why?”

I didn’t even realize when the question slipped out. My mother’s eyes went wide as she knew my schooling skills had been activated. Luckily for me, my father was already distracted by a conversation elsewhere, leaving me with a clear shot.

“I’m older than the girl,” the woman snapped. “Am I supposed to sit on a wet chair?”

“Why not?”

The shock was universal. The guests, the husband, and even the waiters stood frozen with their trays. Only my mother remained in a state of silent prayer, the same one that worked when a church warden tried me last Sunday. But this is not church, and the prayer was not going to save madam today.

Somebody’s mother needed a crash course in home training, and who better to deliver than the Number One Distributor of Home Training in West Africa?

“You don’t have respect,” she began to rant. “When I was your age, I never spoke back to my elders. If the ground was wet, I would lie down so my elders could walk without their shoes getting soiled!”

No vex, add am to your CV.

I didn’t say that out loud. I knew my father would eventually hear of it.

Instead, I smiled. “I’m sorry you had to go through that, but does that mean you should take revenge on us? Is my sister the one who turned you into a floor mat?”

At this point, cans dropped, and spoons returned to plates. The waiters were watching in 4K, and their salaries weren’t large enough to lure them back to work. I was using weaponized empathy, and I was winning. I could see her skin crawling as her nostrils expanded and deflated.

“I’m sorry you went through that.” The phrase hit her like a physical insult. She was fuming, and I enjoyed it, maybe a bit more than I had expected.

I gave her a full, complimentary schooling. I told her to ask for a tissue or napkin instead of ordering someone to stand “just because she was older”.

I made her understand that my sister’s jeans were not rags, and she wasn’t going to use them to mop any chair. If Madam was too human to sit on a wet seat, then my sister was just as human – even more so in my eyes.

In all this, I still blame the husband. The man did not defend his wife or even apologize for her conduct. He just sat quietly and allowed me to deliver a full curriculum for free.

I’ll probably get in trouble with my dad once we leave, but for now? My sister is still in her dry seat, and I have successfully retired a founding member of NAUPA.

I’m currently on my third plate of food—Okpa, by the way. And honestly, stolen peace of mind tastes better than jollof.

My only concern is the way this woman is eyeing me. If my name enters her coven’s register tonight, just know I died for the cause.

Make free home training no go cost me my life, ejo.
